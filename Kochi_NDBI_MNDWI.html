Built-up and Surface-Water Change in Kochi

A satellite-based analysis of urban expansion and surface-water stability using Sentinel-2 imagery over the Kochi Corporation area, comparing the same January–March window in 2018 and 2025.

Kochi is a coastal city in Kerala, India, defined by a distinctive network of backwaters, canals, and islands that shape its urban form. Like many growing Indian metros, it faces pressure to expand — but unlike inland cities, expansion here has to negotiate an unusually water-rich landscape. This analysis asks a simple question: between 2018 and 2025, did Kochi grow into its water bodies, or did it densify on dry land?

To answer this, we use two satellite-derived indices — NDBI for built-up surfaces, and MNDWI for water — computed over the same seasonal window in both years. Both indices are cheap to compute, comparable across years, and interpretable. They tell complementary stories about the same landscape.

01What Are NDBI and MNDWI?

Remote sensing indices are simple arithmetic combinations of satellite bands, designed to make a specific surface type stand out. Both indices used here follow the same underlying idea: contrast one wavelength a material reflects strongly against another it reflects weakly, then normalise the result to a −1 to +1 range.

NDBI (Normalized Difference Built-up Index), proposed by Zha et al. (2003), combines the shortwave-infrared band (SWIR1) with the near-infrared band (NIR): (B11 − B8) / (B11 + B8). Built-up surfaces — concrete, asphalt, roofs — reflect strongly in SWIR and weakly in NIR, producing positive values; vegetation and water produce negative ones. The value behaves like a per-pixel "concrete-ness score."

MNDWI (Modified Normalized Difference Water Index) has a longer history. McFeeters (1996) first exploited the near-infrared (NIR) and green bands of Landsat MSS data to propose the Normalized Difference Water Index (NDWI), which effectively highlighted water and suppressed soil and vegetation — but it failed to separate water from built-up areas, since concrete and water both reflect little NIR. Xu (2006) addressed this by replacing NIR with SWIR1, giving the Modified NDWI: (B3 − B11) / (B3 + B11). Water absorbs almost all SWIR while reflecting green light, so it scores strongly positive; built-up and dry land score negative. MNDWI is now the standard water index for urban areas. (Jiang et al., 2021, extended this line of work further with automated water indices designed for Sentinel-2 imagery, including handling of mountain shadows.)

Known limitations

  • Resolution mismatch: NDBI uses B8 (10 m) and B11 (20 m); MNDWI uses B3 (10 m) and B11 (20 m). Earth Engine resamples B11 to 10 m, introducing a small approximation.
  • Bare soil confusion: dry soil can produce positive NDBI values similar to built-up surfaces, occasionally inflating the built-up signal in newly cleared areas.
  • Water–shadow confusion: MNDWI can misclassify deep terrain shadows as water in hilly scenes — less of an issue in Kochi's flat coastal terrain.
  • Seasonal sensitivity: indices shift with soil moisture, rainfall, and vegetation phenology. Comparing different seasons produces noise; comparing the same season produces signal.
  • Mixed pixels: at 10 m resolution, a single pixel may straddle a rooftop, a tree, and a road, producing an average value that describes none of them well.

02Study Area — Kochi Corporation

The analysis covers the administrative boundary of the Kochi Municipal Corporation, Kerala's largest urban local body. Kochi's geography is defined by the Vembanad backwater system — a network of lagoons, canals, and islands that surrounds and penetrates the city. Roughly 76 wards make up the corporation, spread across the mainland, the islands (Willingdon, Vypin, Mulavukad), and the southern peninsula.

The corporation boundary and ward polygons used in this analysis were sourced from the Datameet Municipal Spatial Data repository on GitHub — a community-curated open dataset of Indian municipal boundaries. Credit goes to the Datameet contributors for maintaining and sharing this resource.

Note on the source data: three ward entries in the Datameet GeoJSON carry polygon areas below 1 m² — clearly digitisation errors rather than genuine administrative units. These wards (Cheralai, Edakochi South, and Kathrukadavu) are excluded from the ward-level statistics below, as too few valid 10 m pixels fall within their boundaries to produce reliable averages. All ward-level results are based on the remaining 68 wards.

03Built-up Change — NDBI

Across the corporation, the mean NDBI rose from −0.0654 in 2018 to −0.0427 in 2025 — an increase of +0.0228. The negative baseline reflects Kochi's unusual land-cover mix: the backwaters, canals, and extensive coconut and mangrove vegetation pull the city-wide average toward the negative side. What matters is the direction of change, not the absolute value.

A +0.0228 shift is small in absolute terms but consistent with the rate of change documented for other growing Indian metros over comparable periods. At the pixel level, this manifests as new red-tinted patches appearing in the 2025 frame — concentrated primarily in the eastern and north-eastern suburbs.

04Water Change — MNDWI

The city-wide mean MNDWI barely moved: −0.1932 in 2018 to −0.1890 in 2025 — a change of only +0.0042. This is essentially flat. The strongly negative baseline is expected: most of the corporation is land, not water, so the area-weighted average will always lean negative even though the backwaters and canals are locally dominant features.

The near-zero change is the more interesting finding. At the corporation scale, surface water appears to have been stable between 2018 and 2025 — gains in some places balanced losses in others. This does not mean no water changed; individual canals may have been filled or widened, and seasonal ponds come and go. But there is no evidence of a systematic, city-wide retreat of water bodies.

Note on omitted wards: three ward entries — Cheralai, Edakochi South, and Kathrukadavu — were excluded from the ward-level statistics. Their polygon areas in the source file are less than 1 m², indicating digitisation errors rather than genuine small wards. Because no 10 m Sentinel-2 pixel fits inside these sliver polygons, reliable averages could not be computed. The 68 remaining wards cover the vast majority of the corporation's inhabited area.

The ward-level pattern reinforces the corporation-wide story. The strongest NDBI increases cluster in the eastern and north-eastern wards — Kakkanad, Edappally, Vyttila — where Kochi's newer residential and commercial development has been concentrated. Wards along the backwater margins show the most variable MNDWI signals, with gains and losses that cancel out at the city scale.

06Observations & Suggestions

What the analysis shows

  • Built-up surfaces expanded measurably across Kochi between 2018 and 2025, with a mean NDBI increase of +0.0228 — consistent with the pace of urban growth reported for comparable Indian metros.
  • At the corporation scale, surface water was stable: the mean MNDWI change was +0.0042, effectively zero. Localized losses and gains balanced out.
  • The combination of rising NDBI and flat MNDWI suggests that growth has been infill-dominated — new construction on already-dry land — rather than reclamation-driven.
  • Spatially, built-up growth concentrates in the eastern and north-eastern suburbs, mirroring Kochi's documented expansion corridors.

What this implies for policy

Key finding: Kochi is densifying on dry land while holding the line on its water bodies at the corporation scale — a notably more sustainable growth pattern than what has been documented in several other Indian coastal cities, where large-scale reclamation has often accompanied urban expansion.
  • Protect what's working: the current balance between built-up growth and water preservation is unusual and worth defending. Continued monitoring can catch any shift toward reclamation early.
  • Focus infrastructure in growth wards: eastern and north-eastern wards are absorbing most of the built-up increase. Targeting transport, drainage, and utility investments there will match demand.
  • Prioritize canal and backwater monitoring: ward-level MNDWI variation is high near the water margins. A finer-scale (5 m) water-mapping effort would clarify which canals are shrinking and which are being maintained.
  • Formalize ward-level monitoring: three wards have digitisation errors in their source polygons. These should be corrected so future analyses can include them.

Limitations

  • Resolution: Sentinel-2's 10–20 m pixels are coarse relative to Kochi's dense mixed land cover; single pixels often straddle multiple surfaces.
  • Single-season comparison: the analysis uses only the Jan–Mar dry season. Monsoon-driven water dynamics are invisible here. A multi-season comparison would round out the picture.
  • Ward-level statistics are means: mean index change per ward is sensitive to outliers, ignores spatial structure, and cannot distinguish "one big project" from "city-wide densification." It should be read as a directional indicator, not a precise estimate.
  • Excluded wards: three wards (Cheralai, Edakochi South, Kathrukadavu) are missing due to polygon errors in the source file. Their real-world area is not reflected in this analysis.

07Methods & Data

Imagery: Copernicus Sentinel-2 Surface Reflectance Harmonized (Level-2A), bands B3 (green), B4 (red), B8 (NIR), B11 (SWIR-1).
Cloud masking: Scene Classification Layer (SCL); pixels marked as cloud, shadow, cirrus, or snow are excluded.
Window: 1 January – 31 March for both 2018 and 2025 (dry season in Kerala).
Compositing: Pixel-wise median of all cloud-free scenes (25 scenes in 2018; 53 scenes in 2025).
Indices: NDBI = (B11 − B8) / (B11 + B8); MNDWI = (B3 − B11) / (B3 + B11).
Change: Per-pixel difference (2025 − 2018) for each index.
Software: Google Earth Engine (Python API), geemap, GeoPandas, PIL.
Source data: Corporation boundary and ward polygons from the Datameet Municipal Spatial Data repository.
